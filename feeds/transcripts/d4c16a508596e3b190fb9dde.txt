Linear was founded to build a better issue tracker. Seven years and $134 million in funding later, its CEO Karri Saarinen published a post titled "Issue tracking is dead." In this episode, we chat with Cristina Cordova , COO of Linear , about how the company navigated that and how its go-to-market changed along the way.
Cristina spent seven and a half years at Stripe, led platform and partnerships at Notion, and was a partner at First Round before joining Linear in 2023. Linear's founders built it after living with legacy issue trackers at Coinbase, Airbnb and Uber, and it spread self-serve through startups like Vercel and Ramp.
When she joined, Linear had about 40 people and one salesperson. Since then it has shipped an MCP server, a platform for building agents into Linear, integrations with Codex, Cursor and GitHub Copilot, and its own Linear Agent. Agents created 3% of Linear's work a year ago. Now it's more than half. Linear passed $100 million in ARR this year with more than 40,000 paying customers and 177% net revenue retention, and in August employees sold $99 million in stock at a $2.5 billion valuation .
We talk about why she stopped chasing enterprise deals three weeks after joining, why Linear has never hired a single SDR, why it won't let an LLM answer support tickets on its own, and what the AI budgets behind $100M-in-months ARR mean for everyone else.
Half the work on Linear is now created by agents
Linear shipped its MCP server in May 2025, and Cristina says it was basically the first in its category. A year and a half later, more than half the issues created in Linear come from agents.
That doesn't mean Linear wants to be a database with an MCP server on top. Cristina thinks SaaS companies settling for that are making a big mistake. If the agent workflows inside your product aren't better than the ones outside it, nobody has a reason to open your app.
So she watches two lines: agent usage and app usage. If agent usage climbs and app usage doesn't, you're becoming a commodity. At Linear, both are up and to the right, because people still come back to the app to see and coordinate the work.

"Issue tracking is dead"
When Karri published "Issue tracking is dead" in March, most of Linear's customers agreed with him. Then they asked the obvious question: so what am I buying? Linear's answer is a product development system built around agent orchestration, where humans stop doing the manual work of filing, labeling, assigning and closing issues.
Customers had been pushing it there. Most of the frontier AI labs run on Linear and wanted it to move faster. Pre-AI companies like Ramp and Coinbase were building their own internal agents and needed an API to plug them into Linear.
Cristina says Linear is lucky it's small enough to cannibalize itself. Google has a much harder problem: put an AI answer above every search result, and fewer people look at the ads.

The AI budget behind $100M in months
The thing speeding up purchases right now, Cristina says, is AI budget. Companies carved out money for AI tools that didn't exist before, and any product that fits inside it skips the usual fight for spend. At some companies that budget is very high.
It's already starting to change. Buyers are signing one-year deals and paid pilots, not three-year contracts, because nobody knows what they'll want in two years. And some overnight-$100M stories are a handful of $30 to $40 million contracts with defense companies or giant law firms. That's a very different business from Linear's 40,000 paying customers on what was, until June, entirely seat-based pricing.
When those one-year deals come up for renewal, we'll find out whose ARR was real.

Linear threw out its 2025 enterprise roadmap
Three weeks after joining in 2023, Cristina crossed "move upmarket to enterprise" off Linear's company goals. It had one salesperson. She went after growth-stage companies that would have to rip out an existing tool instead, the same way Stripe won new businesses long before it won switchers.
Linear finally declared an enterprise push in 2025, with a roadmap full of features for large organizations. Then agents happened. Linear built an MCP server, an agents platform with a whole new set of APIs, and integrations with Codex, Cursor and GitHub Copilot. None of it was planned, and a lot of the enterprise work never got done.
Cristina chose to race ahead anyway. Competitors waited, and enterprises started putting Linear on their shortlists.

No SDRs, no forward deployed engineers
Linear has never hired a single SDR. Cristina thinks the qualification call wastes the customer's time: they want a demo, not questions about their budget. AEs do their own outbound, with tooling to keep it personal.
It doesn't do forward deployed engineering either. Linear should be easy enough to adopt that nobody needs access to your systems. Solutions engineers sell alongside enterprise AEs instead, sometimes building a working ServiceNow integration overnight to show a prospect it can be done. Cristina adds that an opinionated tool gives agents cleaner data than one where every team defines "bug" differently.
Support stays in-house, and Linear won't let an LLM answer tickets on its own. AI drafts replies and a human decides what goes out. Customers sometimes assume the replies are AI because they arrive so fast. In three and a half years, Cristina has never had an escalation over a bad support experience.
Jump to the sales team discussion →
Everyone wants outcome-based pricing
Cristina would love a model like Intercom's Fin, which charges for each support conversation its AI resolves. It's harder for Linear. What is auto-triaging a bug to the right team worth when one company thinks that's the PM's job and another doesn't?
Until someone cracks that, Linear charges for what costs it money and keeps the rest free. Seats stay. Linear Agent chat is free even though it runs on frontier models. In coding sessions, users pick OpenAI or Anthropic models and harnesses, and Linear passes through the published API price plus a small markup. Loops run on AI credits.
Linear doesn't start charging for things that used to be free. New AI features launch as free betas with notice that pricing is coming, and prices start high and come down rather than the other way around.
Jump to the pricing discussion →
Why not hire 100 salespeople?
Cristina says Linear runs on about half the headcount of companies at its stage and revenue. A sales team bigger than the rest of the company would have cost it the trust engineering now has in sales.
From Stripe she brought a 40% rule: at least 40% of the company should be engineering, product, design and data. If sales wanted more people, it helped recruit engineers first. Linear has no hard rule, but one of the first things she built was a headcount spreadsheet by function, and she still watches the ratio.
Hiring is slow on purpose. Candidates do a work trial on a real problem. Hers was writing Linear's go-to-market plan for the next six to twelve months, which then became her job. At about 170 people, the only C-suite is the founders and her.
