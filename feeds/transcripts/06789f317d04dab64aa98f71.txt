New podcast episode between Ege Erdil and Daniel Kokotajlo. I thought this was going to be a debate, but they ended up agreeing on a lot more than I expected.
Their main point of disagreement is how likely it is that we get some intelligence explosion that gives us AIs more competent than any human expert before 2029. Ege puts this possibility at around 20%, whereas Daniel thinks it’s higher than 50%.
They agree that there is not a huge difference in probability, and that, in either case, the situation is crazy enough that our civilization really needs to grapple with how the world might be transformed, and we need a plan that actually makes sense.
In particular, we should stop antagonizing China if we think some kind of global treaty will be needed to make superintelligence go well.
There was another important crux about how well we should expect future AIs (that swamp humans in population size and cognitive power) to treat us, and how relevant it is that current AIs, by and large, seem to be pretty nice.
We close off with predictions for 2027. Daniel, of course, expects superintelligence next year. Ege’s median for full automation is 2040, but by next year he expects AIs that can beat any video game on Steam, and which can control robots to get simple physical tasks (like folding laundry) done at roughly human speed.

Dwarkesh Patel
Today, I’m joined by Ege Erdil and Daniel Kokotajlo . Ege is a researcher at Google DeepMind. Daniel is one of the authors at the AI Futures Project , which published AI 2027 and AI 2040 .
I wouldn’t call this exactly a debate, because I hope it’ll be more of a discussion. But there is a set of key disagreements you guys have, which leads one of you, Daniel, to believe that AI is likely to kill us all within the next few years, and Ege, you to believe that will not happen. I feel like a big crux in this conversation is just timelines. How soon do you expect extreme superintelligence?
I’ve articulated, and Paul has articulated in the last episode , why there could be extreme superintelligence soon. Ege, why do you think that’s unlikely?
Ege Erdil
The main difference between me and Daniel, and also between me and Paul, is just how much you believe in the software-only singularity .
This is the idea that you have a fixed pool of compute and a roughly fixed amount of data that is at least not synthetic. Then what happens is that you get better AIs. Those AIs are able to use that pool of resources way more efficiently to make progress on AI research, and you get this feedback loop that at least goes for many orders of magnitude before it fizzles out. Then that improvement is so big that you get, I don’t know, 10 years of progress in six months or whatever, something like this.
I think the evidence we have is not sufficient to give high credence to that. Maybe that’s 10-20% likely to happen over the next three years. That’s probably the biggest disagreement, where Daniel thinks that’s over 50% likely.
Dwarkesh Patel
Why do you think it’s more than 50% likely that there will be extreme superintelligence within the next few years, whereas Ege thinks that’s 10-20% likely?
Daniel Kokotajlo
First of all, I don’t think that we will probably all be dead in a couple years. The thing I would say is, unless we substantially slow down the AI companies, then probably we will lose control and there will be misaligned AIs running the show in a couple years. It seems like we’re getting close to fully automating the AI research process . That would be the main claim. How close? It seems like it’s going to happen in 2027. Maybe not, maybe 2028, maybe 2029, but I would say it’s slightly more likely than not in 2027.
Once you have fully automated the AI research process, things will probably accelerate. More likely than not, they will accelerate a lot, as Ege was describing, although I do think there’s some uncertainty there.
Then I think that a lot of the limitations of current AI systems, such as the data inefficiency or whatever, will melt away relatively quickly as progress accelerates dramatically. You’ll end up with AIs that are able to flexibly learn quickly from all sorts of experience. You might call them top-expert-dominating AIs , perhaps. Top-expert-dominating AIs are effectively superintelligence as far as I’m concerned.
Dwarkesh Patel
Here’s a naive question. We go back five years and we have AI systems that can’t even hold a conversation. Now we have systems that are solving Millennium Prize Problems . They’re pretty good coders, et cetera.
If we just go another five years of progress at this pace, do you think it’s likely that that alone gives us AIs that are competitive with human experts? If we’re looking forward five years, do we even need that much RSI ?
Ege Erdil
We might not, but I think it’s unclear. If we don’t get any RSI, my median timeline to everything being automated and humans having nothing to do — they’ve been completely automated — is something like 15-20 years from now. That’s a median timeline. So in the next five years, you still have to give it 20% or something that it could happen. I think that’s a very live possibility.
While there are disagreements about software-only singularity and similar things, in some sense those disagreements don’t matter that much. All I’m saying is, Daniel is saying something is 50-60% likely to happen over the next three years, and I’m saying it’s 20% likely. That question might be interesting. But even if it’s only 20% likely, if the situation is very bad, you should still do something about it.
So even though it’s maybe a larger disagreement numerically, I don’t think it’s a significant disagreement qualitatively.
0:04:31 – Why Ege thinks it’s too early to pause
Daniel Kokotajlo
So the reason why you’re working at Google instead of protesting outside of Anthropic, which is a practical difference between our behavior, is less to do with the probabilities on the different scenarios and more to do with… Well, what actually? I’m curious.
Ege Erdil
Several things. In this particular question of whether a pause is a good idea or not, I’m worried about any plan that involves us acting on risks before we have seen some significant version of the costs in the real world. Right now, the thing people always cite in these discussions, since some number of months ago, is the Hugging Face incident . But I personally think the Hugging Face incident didn’t cost that much and was a pretty small incident in the grand scheme of things. It was much less significant than a plane crash in terms of the cost imposed.
I do see it as analogous to if you have a new technology, like nuclear reactors or aircraft or whatever, and you haven’t actually seen anything significant go wrong, but you have all these theoretical arguments for why things might go wrong, and you’re intervening on the basis of that… I’m just suspicious about that approach in general.
The thing I’m challenging when I say that is the view that the way you get aligned AIs is to have some theoretical breakthrough in alignment. Nothing in machine learning has worked like that. Machine learning is not a field that has large theoretical breakthroughs.
There have been no large theoretical breakthroughs over the past 10 years. All of them have been incremental. You stack this compute multiplier on top of this compute multiplier, and you get this efficiency gain. It’s just very incremental. There are a lot of dimensions that you optimize at once. That’s the way you make them more capable.
So I don’t see why you would assume anything different about the way we would make them more aligned.
Dwarkesh Patel
This is maybe a key disagreement between you guys. Are there tractable interventions that we could make? Specifically, are those interventions more tractable if we get more time to make them, interventions that are likely to increase the value of what these AI systems do and how they treat us?

Daniel Kokotajlo
Something like that. We have uncertainty, but yeah.
Dwarkesh Patel

Daniel Kokotajlo

It’s not going fast enough, but it is being made. Each additional year of time that we buy, there’s additional progress, which cuts away at that probability mass of the bad outcomes happening.
The second thing I would say is just general awareness, what you might call AGI-pilling of society and of government. This might actually be the most important thing. Most people just don’t really take this stuff seriously, don’t really understand what’s happening or what’s coming. That seems broadly bad to me. We’d probably be in a better situation if more people in general understood what was coming.
I think that process is happening. It might even be happening exponentially. Every year that goes by, more people learn more, more people pay attention, more people start getting involved.
Ege Erdil
It’s just very difficult without tangible things that the technology does in the world to wake people up to its potential. For example, if we pause at the current level of capabilities, let’s assume we do that. Yes, this process of people waking up continues slowly. d broadly throughout the public.
The public doesn’t really like AI. They don’t really like data centers and stuff. But that’s not because they realize what’s going to happen. They just have a much more myopic distaste for it. If we pause now, then people are still not going to understand what’s at stake. The public conversation about this will still be very poor because it will be operating from bad premises.
There’s an advantage to getting the capabilities to a point where it’s evident to everyone that if we keep going from there on, the world will be totally transformed. Because doing this through ordinary persuasion is extremely difficult.
You might get some kind of pause politically, but it would be supported by factions who don’t actually agree with your worldview. They would support it for some other reason. They don’t like big tech companies. They don’t like the fact that all these companies are constructing all these data centers that the local communities they’re in don’t support. It would be for some other reason and not for the reason that you think is most important.
Daniel Kokotajlo
I technically agree with you, but in spirit disagree. Maybe one way of modeling it is something like: for each new AI capability that appears, there are some rapid updates that people make immediately just upon learning that that capability exists. But then there’s a whole bunch of additional updates that get made as that capability gets more deployed into the world and so forth.
Right now capability progress is moving fast enough that we still haven’t nearly exhausted the gains in awareness that would come from just deploying the current capabilities more. If we had a pause where we continue to deploy the current capabilities but not make them smarter, we would continue to see significant gains in awareness over the course of several years. Then it would taper off after more than that.
I do think that things are going fast enough now that to a first approximation, the wake-up is a function of serial time rather than a function of the capabilities. Although there’s some function of the capabilities.
Another thing I would say too is that a lot of the people who have anti-AI opinions don’t
my worldview and don’t have anti-AI opinions for the same reasons as me. But that’s also changing to a larger extent. We’ve seen multiple senators and congresspeople come out and say, “AI could kill us all,” and, “The AIs could go rogue,” and things like that. That’s mostly happened just in the last year.
So that too is trending in what I would consider the right direction, where people are waking up and understanding the magnitude of the situation more. I don’t think it’s trending fast enough in the right direction. I don’t think that it’s going to be where it needs to be in 12 months, but in 24 months maybe, or in 36 months maybe.
Ege Erdil
I agree with the point that if you pause now, you will still get an increase in awareness for a couple more years. But given the distance between where we are now and where I think people need to get, given the implications of technology, I think that’s just not going to happen if you pause at the current level of capabilities.
It might be enough. You might have the view that the current level of support and current political coalitions or whatever are sufficient to get a pause to happen. Then that has the benefits that you care about, that it slows things down, even though it’s not done with a complete understanding of what’s going to happen and it’s done for different motivations. It’s a defensible position.
I’m just worried in the longer term that if we as a civilization just insist on being irrational about this topic, then we will just make a lot of bad choices, a lot of choices that don’t make that much sense. It’s important that people understand that in some ways, the world we’ve been in is going to no longer exist in some sense.
Even if the outcome is very good for us, if you get Daniel’s ideal outcome or something like that, that world is still completely different from today’s world. People don’t realize. Maybe if they realize, they’ll be like, “Wait, we don’t want that world. That doesn’t fit our preference. We want some other world.”
But if there is never an explicit conversation that has the correct factual premises about what’s going to happen, then we will just not be in control of the situation. Because even though in some sense we are in control, in the sense that all the relevant decisions, at least for a while, are being made by humans, they are not being made as a result of some rational deliberation.
I’m really worried about that. Some random thing is going to happen, and we won’t control what happens. It will not be the output of any process that makes any sense. Then we will have to live with it. That’s very scary.
Daniel Kokotajlo
That’s a reason to go slower in AI alignment.
Ege Erdil
It’s a reason to go slower after sufficiently many people are aware of the potential of technology.
Daniel Kokotajlo
And you think it’s too early for that?
Ege Erdil
I think it’s too early.

Then what about, instead of going to a complete pause, going slower? Say we do the thing where we restrict AI R&D compute to 10% or 5% of the company’s compute. This slashes the overall pace of progress by a factor of two or three or something.
Perhaps you would agree then that because you are still making forward progress, you are still going to get the levels of awareness eventually.
Ege Erdil
A couple things about that. First of all, I don’t know how to get international coordination for this. Given what we’re currently doing, it’s going to be very hard because the US is being quite adversarial against China right now, especially with recent events. That just seriously undermines the possibility of getting any coordination. It’s actually very myopic and dangerous. They really have to stop doing that.
Daniel Kokotajlo
One of the nice things about the 10% idea is that it would still leave the US companies with about as much compute on AI R&D as the Chinese companies. Also, because a lot of Chinese progress is just coming from the US, you wouldn’t even need a deal to do this level of pacing.
Ege Erdil
For now, but that’s because China is behind on a bunch of the industrial tech that’s required to produce a lot of compute. For example, they don’t have access to EUV machines and stuff like this. The US has been able to get the Netherlands to successfully export control those and so on. But given China’s rate of economic growth and industrial progress, these are temporary advantages. So it gives us some time.
China is not going to be able to catch up in five years, probably. So if you think five years is enough, then you can get five years with a method like this. But if you think we need 15 years or something like that, then I become more pessimistic.
Dwarkesh Patel
Or let alone some global agreement. In the limit, it is worth discussing. Unless you think you’re just going to literally outcompete every single country.
Daniel Kokotajlo
I agree that ultimately, if we want to have a good future, we’re probably going to need to make some sort of treaty or deal with China and other countries, something like that.
Ege Erdil
If you’re really worried about that risk, there has to be coordination with them. There’s no way to avoid that. You can try to delay things. You can have a five-year lead. You have five additional years. That buys you something. But if you’re really worried about that risk, they could try to build it in five years. It’s not that long.
Any prospects of coordination with them, I think, are being severely damaged by the policy of the US government towards China, and also, to some extent, of Anthropic towards China.
Daniel Kokotajlo
I agree with that.
Ege Erdil
This is very short-sighted. It’s because people don’t understand the stakes of the technology. The awareness that this is a world-transforming technology that completely changes the condition of civilization is just not there.
We have to figure out a way to deal with it, and there’s no way to adequately deal with it without some kind of coordination. And people are torpedoing the prospects for doing that for some very short-sighted, short-term gain of, “Oh, we get a couple years’ additional lead.” It doesn’t make sense, what people are doing. So I think they’re operating from a very different view of what the technology will be like, which I think is going to have bad consequences.
This is another thing I think is not being discussed, even in communities where people are trying to think of, “How do we win? How do we get the safe AI first?” Gwern had an interesting comment about this , I think on LessWrong , where he asked the question: let’s say the US wins. We get our superintelligence. Then what?
What do we do? Do we do some crazy operation to try to overthrow the Chinese government or something like that? If they think that’s what the US is going to do, what are they going to think of any attempt to try to coordinate with them to get a slowdown? They’re inherently going to be extremely suspicious of any such attempt if they think that is the plan.

People still don’t, as far as I can see, just buy this premise. Humans are physical objects. We have some capacities. The brain has some capacities. It’s just a physical object. That argument is very straightforward. It doesn’t really hinge on these details about exactly when it happens, but people just don’t believe it.
It seems to me like this very deep-rooted irrationality that is tied to maybe lingering notions of the human soul or whatever. There’s a lot of this going on where people don’t want to confront the reality of what’s happening.
The basic thing is that there is a massive transformation that’s going to happen. And you need to understand it’s going to happen if you want to talk about, “What do we do? What do we change? How do we adapt to it? What is our plan?” We have no plan right now. There’s nothing.
The fact that people will be superseded as the things that produce economic value, that has dramatic implications. People don’t think that’s going to happen, so they’re doing nothing about that either. For the AI thing broadly, what I would say… There’s this quote from Nietzsche. He wrote, “ God is dead , but given the way of man, for many thousands of years there may be caves in which his shadow will be shown, and we have to vanquish his shadow too.”
I think that is what AI is doing. We have all these almost quasi-religious beliefs about the human mind, and all of them are going to be undermined. Dwarkesh Patel

Daniel Kokotajlo
Sounds like a really dangerous plan to me.

The point I’m making is more narrow, for what’s happening right now. A lot of hiring processes were designed for a time when experience was very important, and I think that’s no longer true. But the processes are not adapting.
That’s the sort of thing I’m talking about, where people are in denial, and them being in denial results in tons of inefficient and bad decisions. As technology advances, the stakes get higher and higher. That’s why I think it’s very important for people to have an accurate assessment of what the technology is capable of.

Even GTA VI or something?

That’s what it will look like when we have these robots. It won’t be that we suddenly have robots and we have this industrial explosion that suddenly takes off. Ege Erdil

Ege Erdil
I’m not sure. The concept of horizon length seems like too much of a leaky abstraction to me here. Often it’s not even easy to understand how these tasks are distributed.
A task can look extremely technically complicated and ambitious: take this program and completely formalize it and prove things about it or whatever. This is the kind of thing a human would take many months on. An agent can often do it in hours.
While a much simpler task like, “I’m going to put you in charge of looking at other people’s changes to this codebase, and make sure you don’t approve something if it looks obviously dumb or if it breaks something,” this kind of thing, they just can’t retain memory. They can’t understand what it means for something to be bad or dangerous to merge.
It’s because there’s too much context. They are bad at arranging information in such a way that they retain all the context, they know what’s relevant and what’s not relevant. There seems to be something missing there, and it’s causing their horizon lengths to be extremely jagged.
On the tasks which are of the second type that I’m talking about, I don’t think we’ve been seeing progress that is 10x a year in horizon length.
Daniel Kokotajlo
This is a live question for me. This is something that’s somewhat crux-y for me. If I were more convinced that they were really not improving that much at a whole range of really important skills, that would lengthen my timelines significantly.
One question I have is, how much hard evidence do we have that this is the case versus just anecdotes? A skeptical-of-this-hypothesis but more bullish hypothesis overall about AI would be that, at any given level of AI capability, there will still be things that they still can’t do. Basically, one thought is, how confident are you that they really aren’t improving that much over the years, versus they are improving a lot, it’s just that there’s a significant order of magnitude or two gap?
Ege Erdil
It’s difficult to name a task where the AIs are not making significant progress, just in capability. That was not true, I think, some number of years ago, but right now it is true.
The difference here is more, do you think the progress in some metric, let’s say horizon lengths or something like that, is 2-3x a year, or is it more like 10x or 100x a year? It’s more a quantitative question as opposed to, are they making progress or not?
For example, in the skill of writing an original novel that is good, I think the progress since last year is definitely not 100x. Probably not even 10x. It might be 3x, but this is very subjective. It’s very hard to measure this.
But with a lot of these things, we don’t have anything like METR’s time horizon benchmark to settle these questions. I’m just having to make these intuitive judgments because we don’t have good benchmarks. So I could be totally wrong about some of this, and if you were to quantify it, it will come out that I was wrong.
With the time horizon thing, I think the issue is that the moment you turn it into a benchmark, it has to be something that the AIs can already do reasonably well enough, that can then become an optimization target, and that makes it relatively easy to make progress on it.
My guess of what would happen for the METR time horizons thing, and I’ve said this, I think, early last year, was that this is basically going to diverge. They’re going to get near infinite time horizon. But there will still be a bunch of just 10-minute tasks that the AIs can’t actually do very well because of just distributional shift . That has basically happened now. They stopped reporting the numbers because it was diverging .
But take some domain in which they can do a 10-minute task now. What is the length of the task that they will be able to do in that domain in one or two or three years? It’s probably just very domain sensitive. So I don’t really have a very good answer to this question, because we don’t have a good benchmark that can settle it.
It’s just my intuitive judgment, based on using the systems, that they seem like they made vast progress in math, probably even more than 100x in terms of time horizon over the last year. In some other domain — like sending emails on my behalf that are not confusing and that people on the other side can understand — I feel like the progress is much, much less.

1:23:38 – Is AI research actually verifiable?
Daniel Kokotajlo
I think this is plausible. My follow-up question then is, to what extent can this be explained by the training environments ? I would imagine that they have a lot of training environments that are of the form, “Prove this theorem or disprove this theorem,” and a lot of training environments that are of the form, “Solve this coding problem,” or, “Write this piece of software that passes these unit tests .” And not many problems that are of the form, “Make a good aesthetic judgment about how to organize clips” or something like that, because it’s harder to set up that sort of environment, and maybe also because it’s less lucrative, although I’m not sure about that.
You work at DeepMind, so do you think that a lot of this jaggedness is basically downstream of just the task distribution? Or do you think that it’s like, “Oh no, I’ve seen examples of the company trying really hard to collect a lot of high-quality data and training environments for a particular type of 10-minute task such as that, and then just still failing to get the AI as good at it”?
Ege Erdil
I can’t speak to what the company is doing internally. What I can say is based on just public evidence. There’s a cost to verification that I think is driving a lot of this.
I don’t really agree with the split that some tasks are verifiable and some tasks are not verifiable. For example, did you produce a video for his podcast that will make Dwarkesh happy? That’s verifiable. You can give the video to him, and he’s like, “What the hell is that?” But the issue is that’s a very expensive way to get feedback.
The difference with math is that math is very cheap to get feedback on. There’s a very cheap verifier. That does have a huge effect, because the way you should think about it is: how efficiently can you sample trajectories that give you the right answer? How fast can you discriminate things that work from those that don’t work? That’s a very fundamental thing for how efficiently you can learn things.
One thing, for example, that makes me more skeptical of software-only singularity type stories is that while I agree that ML research is a verifiable domain, in practice a lot of the verification is quite expensive. This is why compute matters so much. That’s why compute is so important for algorithmic progress.
You can have innovations at small scales that fail at large scales. You can have innovations that only work at large scales that you can never confirm by experiments at small scales because they just completely fail. You get false positives and false negatives in both directions. If you want to collect a bunch of data about this to train the models to have better taste, that needs a lot of compute as well, and probably more and more as you scale up the size of the experiments.
That makes me not put as much credit on the arguments that say, “Look, we made a decade of progress in one or two months in math , and therefore we should expect a decade of progress in ML research.” If you think of the dollar cost of verifying some idea in ML research and compare it to the dollar cost of verifying a math proof, it’s many, many orders of magnitude apart.
Seeing if some proof of the Riemann hypothesis in Lean is correct or not, you can do that on your personal computer. It’s very easy. There’s a linear time algorithm for checking proofs, basically. While if you’re trying to figure out, “I have this fancy change to this optimizer, does that really give me a compute efficiency when I’m pre-training or not?”, it’s very hard to settle that question. You can do small-scale experiments. It can look like it works, but then you scale it up and, “Oh wait, it doesn’t work. What’s going on?” Then it’s very confusing.
To me, it looks like the cost of verification is much, much higher. That’s probably, honestly, a key reason to expect that domain to be significantly more difficult to automate. It’s not that humans are very good at it, because I think humans are also bad at ML research, just like we’re bad at math.
It’s just that AIs, due to their current sample inefficiency and stuff like that, really benefit from being able to get just a ton of data in there. That is very contingent on there being a very cheap verification procedure for whether you did the right thing or not. The more expensive that is, the slower you make progress, basically. So that’s my rough take about this.
Dwarkesh Patel
There is some argument about generalization with respect to the cost. Even in math, presumably the way that these models became good at, say, being able to accommodate billions of tokens — or was it hundreds of millions of tokens — in order to solve Navier-Stokes across a swarm of thousands of agents was not through many other tasks where 1,000 agents collaborated in order to solve a math proof and spent tens of millions of dollars’ worth of tokens to do so. There was some generalization to a much more ambitious kind of assignment from the math training that they underwent.
Then the question is, will you see that kind of generalization in AI research? Now, of course, they’re much more sample efficient than humans, so you can’t use this as an exact analogy.
But my understanding is that researchers who were in the field in, say, 2020, when there was much less compute being used, at a level of compute that can be part of the training process now, are good at doing AI research now. There are some lessons they learned from training GPT-2 - or GPT-3 -size models that are helping them do good research at the current frontier. So it does seem like you should expect to see this kind of generalization.
I agree that you could not cheaply and consistently verify at the scale at which you’d need the AI researchers operating.
Ege Erdil
I totally agree with that. You should expect to see a lot of generalization. In fact, we have seen a lot of generalization, as you said, just based on Navier-Stokes.
The other thing I would say is that there is such a thing as general intelligence . With these base models , I think pre-training loss is a pretty good proxy for that thing. It does just predict, in a bunch of ways, “Will the model understand some subtlety about your request and interpret it correctly even though it was ambiguous?” and all kinds of things we normally associate with intelligence. It just does predict it.
Obviously the entire reason this field works at all is because this concept exists. Otherwise this wouldn’t work. I totally agree with that. For example, there was this plot that was published by Anthropic about pre-training compute multiplier sizes or something like that. They didn’t report actual sizes, but there was a plot published in their IPO roadshow .
I have some difficulty interpreting that plot. I can’t exactly say what I think is wrong with it. I think they’re not disclosing a lot of information that is relevant for thinking about whether you trust their judgment of what’s going on or not. I can say, based on the information I have, that I don’t trust their judgment of what’s going on, but I can’t explain why. That’s a little bit unsatisfying.
Daniel Kokotajlo
Does that mean you think that there hasn’t been an acceleration in pre-training progress over the last year at Anthropic?
Ege Erdil
I would say that question is poorly posed. I don’t think I can elaborate more than that without revealing something. You can say there has been and there hasn’t. It depends on how you interpret the question.
Dwarkesh Patel

Daniel Kokotajlo
It’s an update to hear that you don’t believe it. So that’s something.
Ege Erdil
I’m not saying I don’t believe it. I’m saying the thing they are reporting is poorly posed. I’m not saying they made up the numbers. I think there is a real graph inside Anthropic that probably looks like that. They didn’t fabricate the numbers. It’s just that I think that doesn’t mean what they are portraying it to mean.
Dwarkesh Patel
I’m guessing what you’re referring to is that there’s some synthetic-data -type compute multiplier that makes it look like the loss on that corpus is higher, but maybe in a way that doesn’t generalize. Of course, I can only speculate, but these are the kinds of things one could think make the pre-training thing misleading.
Ege Erdil
I can’t comment on that. But this is a trade-off. This is something people have to decide. You can decide people should be more transparent. You can have a regulation that companies have to be more transparent , they have to publish a bunch of information. But then you have to accept the fact that anything that is published, China also finds out.
Dwarkesh Patel
Well, China’s finding out anyways. At least we get to find out.
Daniel Kokotajlo
Yeah, I accept that fact. They’re probably finding out anyway.
Ege Erdil
Right now, there’s a lot of leakage because security posture is so bad. But in a world where you also want to improve the security posture, doing both of these interventions at once defeats a lot of the point of improving security, if you’re also going to get the labs to publish all of this information anyway.
Maybe you can defend the weights , and the weights are not going to get stolen . But basically everything else, all the algorithmic innovations and so on, they would very easily just reconstruct from the public information. That is a cost. It might be a cost worth paying, depending on how dire you think the situation is in a bunch of ways.
Daniel Kokotajlo
I think it is worth paying.
Ege Erdil
But then the proper way to do that… Again, that’s why I think improving the quality of public discourse, not about these specific details, but just about the general fact of the transformativeness of the technology and the size of the impact it will have, is so important. If the public takes this seriously, then this is a decision that could be made by the government. Then it could become a regulation. We could just accept that, “Yes, we understand there’s a cost here, but given the stakes of the situation, we think we should pay this cost.”
For example, Anthropic doesn’t believe that. Even though they also are aware of the stakes, they just don’t think that cost is worth paying. I think that’s what’s going on. I don’t think they’re necessarily being self-interested about that. They just have a real belief that this would be worse for the world if they made that information public. But Daniel doesn’t
that belief.
If there’s no public discourse about it, it’s impossible to get the companies to actually publish this stuff. There has to be some external thing that comes in and says, “Look, you guys can think that, but we are aware of the stakes here. We have decided that it is important for this information to be public.” Unless that can happen, anyone can say, “Look, I think it’s better for the world, and I’m just going to do what I think is best.” There’s no regulation, there are no rules.
That’s a very bad situation. Not because I necessarily think that the decision to keep it secret is a wrong decision, but because the process that’s outputting that outcome is bad. As I said, there should be a plan. We should understand what the stakes are. There should be rational public deliberation about this topic, and people should come to a conclusion: “Yes, we want this,” or, “No, we don’t want this.” That has not happened at all.
This is a unilateral decision that is being taken by some people who might be right, might be wrong. They have certain preferences. Even the government, I think, is not really playing a large role in that, because they don’t understand the stakes.
The government might play a role in something like, “Oh, we are worried about Chinese cyber attacks , and we want to have capable cyber models. We don’t want that to get leaked.” Or they might worry about specific concrete risks. But if you don’t appreciate the total transformative potential of the technology, that it’s going to totally change the human condition, for example, then you’re just unaware of the stakes. You’re not going to make the right decisions.
