We last spoke with Evan about commoditizing compute . At AI Engineer New York, we went deeper: what does it take to physically deliver a GPU contract, and what has to be true before a market in compute can become liquid?
From selling a room to running the hotel
Evan describes SF Compute’s expansion with a hotel analogy. A customer wants a room, then asks for cleaning, room service, and security. Eventually you discover that delivering the room means operating the hotel. SF Compute began by trying to sublease a cluster; the company has since built much of the operating capacity of a cloud to deliver the compute traded on its market.
That work is physical settlement: the buyer gets usable compute, rather than simply a cash payment against an index. It brings the physical constraints of a cluster into the market itself. Can a workload be interrupted? How quickly can it recover from storage? Which GPU generation, networking and software configuration does it need? Those details affect both the contract and the price.
Making compute tradeable
Rather than waiting for every GPU cluster to become interchangeable, SF Compute matches orders with different constraints. Evan argues that this makes trading possible while the technology changes, at the cost of spreading liquidity across more variants. We also discuss spot and forward pricing, batch versus live inference, and why an index can become disconnected from the cost of actually buying compute.
Independence is central to Evan’s case for a market operator. He argues that companies with large hardware or inference businesses have incentives that make it harder to run a neutral exchange. We also get into the unglamorous engineering: visiting sites, designing clusters, running the backplane, and making infrastructure programmable. Near the end, he describes the ability to subdivide InfiniBand fabric on the fly as one example.
