New episode with military historian Si Sheppard .
The conquests of the Aztec and Inca empires might be the most shocking events in human history.
Hernan Cortés and some hundreds of Conquistadors landed on a new continent they knew basically nothing about, and within 2 and a half years, conquered the 6 million strong Aztec empire.
Then, a decade later, Francisco Pizarro did the same thing to an Incan Empire of some 10 million inhabitants.
In both cases, the Conquistadors consistently beat native armies while being literally 100x (or even in some cases 1000x) outnumbered - and in many cases, did so without taking a single casualty.
And they did this with 99% of their military forces being made up of the very natives they were trying to subjugate.

But this would not be the first time an alien force whose motives the defenders barely understand use more advanced technology and diplomatic cunning to outmaneuver and then take over a much larger and long established existing order.

Dwarkesh Patel
Today I’m chatting with professor and military historian Si Sheppard . I want to talk to you about the conquest of the Aztec and the Inca Empires because these are some of the most shocking events in human history, unparalleled among all the military and diplomatic achievements ever made. A few hundred conquistadors show up to a new continent they know nothing about. Within a matter of years, they subjugate empires which have millions of inhabitants in them. In the process of doing so, they are often winning battles where they are outnumbered 100 to 1, 1,000 to 1, and in many cases not even taking a single casualty. They’re doing this while upwards of 99% of their fighting forces are made up of the very natives that they’re going to subjugate.
I wanted to have you on to talk about something this episode. We’re not going to focus on it, we’re mostly talking about history here, but people think of AI takeover as this crazy sci-fi scenario but this would not be the first time in history that an alien force, with motivations that the natives don’t understand, is able to use diplomatic cunning and technological advantages to outmaneuver and then take over a much larger and well-established order. I wanted to have you on to understand what happened here.
First, I would love to hear the stories. What did Cortés do? What did Pizarro do? How did these conquests actually go down?
Si Sheppard
Thank you. It’s a great pleasure to be here. Yes, we’re talking about the birth of our modern world, which played out between 600 to 500 years ago and created the modern globalized community that we all participate in today.
It began in the 15th century when Europe, backed up against the wall of the Atlantic, under immense pressure from external forces and cut off from the core economic power bases of the East — the sources of the spice and silk that had driven the global economy historically throughout Eurasia — was finally forced to innovate. Key names like Prince Henry the Navigator of Portugal work on turning the Atlantic Ocean from a barrier into a superhighway. Generational knowledge accrued from captains reporting on tides and wind currents and whatever land they’ve managed to reconnoiter, building out the map as they go along, enables them to find a way around the southern tip of Africa directly to India and therefore cut out the middleman.
The Spanish, meanwhile, are heading west. We all know the story of Christopher Columbus : “In 1492, he sailed the ocean blue.” That was entrepreneurship. This was an expedition to arrive at the Oriental markets by traveling west. Of course, his figures were completely wrong. He was saved by the fact that, unknown to him, there was an entire continent in the place where he thought Japan, China, and the Spice Islands would be. Initially, that was perceived, ironically, as a disappointment. But over time, it became increasingly apparent this was a source of potential in land, in wealth, and in people that could be exploited.
A key turning point happened in 1511 when an ambitious Spanish nobleman, de Velázquez , seizes control of Cuba. He sends out from this power base expeditions exploring the coast of what we would call Mesoamerica, Central America. Later in 1518, he assigns that task to a man with whom he’s had a somewhat tempestuous relationship, a man named Hernán Cortés . He’d arrived in the New World in Cuba in 1511. He had some legal training, so he served as a notary and worked his way up to being alcalde , which is chief magistrate, on Cuba. But he had much greater ambition.
He takes this warrant from the governor and runs with it. He begins recruiting men to a much wider and much more ambitious goal: not just to explore the coast, but to penetrate into the interior, to find out what’s there, and to seize control of it. When the governor realizes what Cortés is up to, he tries to stop him. There’s this great moment on 18th November 1518. He arrives at the waterfront just as Cortés gets into a boat to go out to his ships offshore. The governor calls out to him, “What are you up to?” Cortés assures him, “Don’t worry. I have this all under control.” But of course, the governor had lost control at that point. Now it was Cortés’s expedition.
This is the background. Again, as you say, he is literally going off the side of the map. He’s entering terra incognita, unknown to Europeans previously. His political antenna, honed by years in the islands, enables him to appreciate at a very early stage that there is a significant imperial power which has hegemony here in the mainland, and that its subject peoples chafe under the authority of whoever this lord or imperial power is.
He arrives in the lands of the Totonacs , who had recently been incorporated into the imperial orbit of a people who call themselves the Mexica — hence Mexico as we know it — but who have come down to history based on their mythical origins in a land called Aztlán as the Aztecs. For simplicity’s sake, that’s what we’ll refer to them as. The Totonacs resented this authority, as all subjugated and subordinated peoples do, and they saw the arrival of these outsiders as a potential opportunity to throw off the authority that had been imposed on them.
Having established this relationship with the Totonacs, Cortés begins to march inland, and he enters the territory of the Tlaxcalans , who, like an autoimmune system, immediately rallied to defeat him, to expel him from their territory. But he fights them to a standstill for such a period of time that the Tlaxcalans and he arrive at a new kind of arrangement. The Tlaxcalans again appreciate his strength, his power, his potential. They swing over to his side and will henceforward serve as guides and reinforcements for his expedition deeper into the interior.
Dwarkesh Patel
Can we pause here and talk about the numbers? How many men had Cortés brought with him? Even the Tlaxcalans had much larger numbers than Cortés did. So even the standstill is an impressive military achievement, especially because he’s fighting without native support. What are the numbers even at this point?
Si Sheppard
A few hundred on Cortés’ side and multiple thousands on the Tlaxcalans’ side. It’s at this point that the critical technological differentials from the Old and New World really begin to come into effect. The classic text which got me invested in this whole scenario in the first place is Jared Diamond ’s book, Guns, Germs, and Steel . He enumerates in the title those critical advantages that the Spaniards had.
Guns in reality weren’t so significant. Once the Native peoples got over the shock value, they appreciated, given the very slow reloading times involved in firearms at that time, that they weren’t so significant as assets on the battlefield. But steel was critical. Local weapons were carved from wood and from stone and from obsidian , that volcanic glass which is razor sharp when honed to a point and quite dangerous as a slashing weapon. But against the steel armor, and especially the steel helmets that the conquistadors were wearing, it was much less effective.
The conquistadors were drawing on military traditions of the Old World that went back generations, incorporating long Toledo steel sword blades. They used those as thrusting weapons to take out indigenous warriors who were coming in with cut-and-slash weapons, which of course open up the side of the body to be taken out very early. The combination of the technology with generations of inherited military training and skill meant that the conquistadors had that critical edge on the battlefield.
Now, they weren’t invincible. Cortés was again playing those mind games. He was taking casualties, and he was careful to bury his dead underneath the houses that they were sheltering in so the Tlaxcalans wouldn’t be aware that they were even having some successes in hand-to-hand combat. It was as much a mind game as it was a physical game.
The ultimate weapon, of course, that the conquistadors brought with them was their horses. I cannot overemphasize how vital the horse was to the conquistadors once they entered into the interior of the American continents. They had the external asset. They had sail power, which enabled them to land anywhere they wanted on the coast. But in the interior, the horse was decisive. There were no native equivalents. The horse originated in North America, but it had subsequently died out. The local peoples had not just no horses, but no beast of burden generally. That was not integrated into their economies, into their ways of life, or into their military.
The Inca , whom we’ll discuss in a moment, did have one, which was the llama . When they campaigned, trailing for miles behind their armies would be these endless baggage trains of pack animals, llamas. The Aztecs had no equivalent. Their only domestic animals were the turkey and the Chihuahua.
Speaking of dogs, the conquistadors also brought these big war mastiffs , these fighting dogs which wore armor and were trained to take down men. Again, imagine the terrifying impact that would have for the indigenous people. But the horse was the critical asset. On attack, you could blast through any native formation which didn’t have not just the weapons, but the training or discipline to be able to stand up to massed shock cavalry. If things were going bad for the conquistadors, they could retreat much faster than, of course, the enemies could chase them.
The Tlaxcalans appreciate all these assets coming together, and they see this as their opportunity. They were the holdouts, the last remaining major entity in Mesoamerica which was still able to go toe-to-toe with the Aztecs. They’d been completely surrounded. The Aztecs were upping the ante every year with these ceremonial flower wars , which were becoming more and more obviously wars of conquest. The Tlaxcalans had been cut off from the coast and from sources of salt. So they were being pushed to the brink. This is a Hail Mary for them. “If we ally with these outsiders, these strangers, who are just few in number after all, we may have some potential way to break out of this strategic trap that has been imposed upon us.”
Si Sheppard
They lean over to the side of Cortés, and they travel with him to Tenochtitlan , the capital, the glittering jewel in the midst of Lake Texcoco , the capital of the Aztec Empire . Again, this is a dramatic moment when Cortés passes through the causeways out into the city and encounters Moctezuma II , the huey tlatoani as he was called, the Great Speaker, the emperor of the Aztec Empire. These two worlds come together at that moment. The interface between them was Cortés’s interpreter/mistress, Doña Marina , Malinche as she became known, who is translating from one side to the other. The dialogue that occurs then is one of those great tipping points in history.
What was the perspective of Moctezuma II? He was aware Cortés was coming. This is his empire. He has envoys and emissaries and scouts and spies everywhere. They’re reporting back to him, “Something has washed up on the shores of our empire, and it is determined to meet with us.”
He initially bargains with Cortés, urging him to leave. He provides him with precious gifts, which of course just inflames Cortés even more. Once he’s got hold of Aztec gold, he’s just determined to go all the way to the center. Moctezuma continues playing this double game. His advisors, his council, urge him not to allow Cortés to proceed to the capital. His own brother, Cuitláhuac , tells him, “If you allow this man inside your house, he will take your house away from you.” But Moctezuma proceeds, and for that he’s been condemned by generations of historians. This was a mistake to bring the conquistadors into the heart of the empire itself.
I actually think that was his best option, whether he was aware of it or not, because it was better for him to have Cortés as his guest within Tenochtitlan than it was to have Cortés, this random element, at loose within his empire, talking to the subject peoples, inflaming them possibly against central authority. He felt perhaps Cortés was what he said he was, an envoy from a great emperor on the far side of the vast ocean. Cortés, again with the mind games, had no warrant to say he was a diplomat or ambassador for anybody. He kept telling anyone who’d listen, “I speak for the mighty Charles V , the Holy Roman Emperor .” Of course, Charles V had no idea who Hernán Cortés was. Cortés, again, is just winging it, just incredible improvisation here. And for a moment… stasis.
The great turning point comes when word arrives from the coast that one of the Aztec regional military officers, on his own initiative, has taken action against the conquistadors left behind on the coast, the ones with the Totonacs, and some of them have been killed. Cortés seizes this as his pretext. He marches into the palace of Moctezuma II, the Aztec huey tlatoani , and tells him that for his own safety, he is now being taken under the protective custody of the Spanish, and he should come with him, or else there will be violence.
This is the moment when Moctezuma’s entire worldview just collapses. Nobody had spoken to him like this before. His psychology just disintegrates at that point, and he meekly allows himself to be led away. He tells his people, “Don’t resist. I don’t want bloodshed. I will pass with these strangers.” Then once Cortés has rounded up the officers from the coast responsible for turning on his men there, he has them, in front of the emperor and the entire court, burned alive, which is a form of punishment totally unknown to the Aztecs. They watch this in grim silence.
Once Cortés has seized the emperor himself, once he has Moctezuma, he already has the empire. He governs through what is increasingly becoming his puppet. Moctezuma seems to have fallen almost under the spell of Cortés, the classic Stockholm syndrome . He gives him a grand tour of the city. Cortés, in again a subtle flex of his power, has brigantines , small sailing boats, built. He takes Moctezuma with him on a sailing cruise on Lake Texcoco, and the emperor’s astonished at this new technology of using the wind to sail on the water.
Having the emperor was the key. The emperor’s mistake wasn’t inviting Cortés into the city. It was making himself vulnerable by staying there also in an adjacent palace. If he had gone to some further location, made some excuse, a pilgrimage or an expedition of some kind, and had ruled through subordinates, Cortés would’ve been baffled. He couldn’t go chasing after the emperor. That would be obviously too provocative. But without the emperor there, Cortés would have had his options extremely limited. I don’t know what he would’ve done in that circumstance. But because Moctezuma stayed in his capital, he made himself the target. Once he was taken, this was the key to controlling the entire empire at that point.
Dwarkesh Patel
Obviously the story continues from there, with La Noche Triste . What people might not appreciate about what happened here, in both the Pizarro story of the Inca Empire which we’ll talk about later, and in the Aztec story, is that in both cases the Spanish were themselves divided and in fact fighting each other. So maybe you should tell the story of how Velázquez sends another expedition to capture and arrest Cortés for going beyond the prerogative of his mission.
Si Sheppard
We left the governor on Cuba, of course, fuming. He rallies another expeditionary force because Cortés is stealing his thunder. He sends this under Pánfilo de Narváez , a larger force than the one Cortés brought with him originally. This lands on the coast. Cortés appreciates he has to move, so he leaves one of his subordinates, the hot-blooded Alvarado , behind in charge in Tenochtitlan, takes as many men as he can to the coast, confronts de Narváez, and defeats him in a minor battle.
Again, diplomacy wins over the entire expeditionary force that the governor had dispatched. He tells them, “My friends, why are we fighting? We have the empire at my disposal. Join with me and we can
in the riches.” So he wins over the vast majority of this new force. It effectively becomes reinforcements, and he marches back to the capital.
He finds the mood has turned against him. The problem with being in charge is you can’t be everywhere. His subordinate, assuming that the Aztecs were mobilizing against him, decided on a preemptive strike. There was a festival. The aristocracy, particularly the warrior caste, were celebrating, and Alvarado just descended on them and massacred everybody . The survivors rally the population. When Cortés returns, he finds himself effectively under siege. The people are so appalled now with the failure of their huey tlatoani that when he tries to pacify them, they throw stones at him. Mortally wounded, he either dies or the Spanish finish him off , because he’s no longer of any use to them.
Now Cortés is trapped in Tenochtitlan. He determines to break out, and that is, as you say, La Noche Triste, the Tragic Night, because so many of his men are left behind. They’re either killed as they try to escape the city across the causeways or forced to retreat back to the palaces, where they’re taken alive and sacrificed subsequently. Cortés gets away with a minority of the troops still at his disposal and makes it back to Tlaxcalan territory.
That was the critical aspect, the two features of the entire campaign. One, he has enough strike power with enough horses to defeat the Aztecs when they try to finish him off, at the Battle of Otumba . They feel he’s on the run. They have the numbers. The conquistadors are dragging their tails between their legs as they flee. Cortés himself is grievously wounded. “We can finish them off.” They had the numbers. They were winning. But again, Cortés identifies where the Aztec general is. He rallies enough horsemen to blast through the Aztec ranks and take out their command and control. The Aztec army disintegrates, and the Spanish are able to flee.
The battlefield advantages, their horses, their steel, were critical, but just as critical was the diplomacy. If the Tlaxcalans had turned on Cortés at that point, he would’ve been finished. Not one Spaniard would’ve survived. None of them would’ve made it back to the coast. But the Tlaxcalans, for their own reasons, not out of any generosity or humanitarian impulse to welcome back the conquistadors, still see, “We are committed to these people and to our alliance.” So they give Cortés shelter. He recovers physically, begins to rebuild his strength, and is determined to go back to Tenochtitlan for round two: “This time we’re not going to be indulging diplomatic niceties.”
The Tlaxcalans have their own perspective on this. If you go to Tlaxcala, the territory today, the governor’s mansion there has murals on the wall, and these depict Cortés and the conquistadors from the Tlaxcalan point of view. It’s fascinating to look at how the relationship is not between a superior and an inferior. It’s between equals. You see them depicted in the same scale, engaged in diplomacy between partners, not between subordinates and dominant figures. The Tlaxcalans drove a hard bargain. “We want territory carved out of the Aztecs. We want our own fortress in Tenochtitlan after this is over.” They saw this as them using the conquistadors, not the other way around, which is very important to bear in mind.
The Aztec Empire from a distance, if you look at a map, seemed impressive. But this was an imperial construct, and not just the remaining independent entities like the Tlaxcalans, like the Tarascans , who we can discuss later, were hostile to the Aztecs. So were so many of their subordinated peoples. The Totonacs on the coast, the Chalco states on the shores of Lake Texcoco, they chafed under Aztec hegemony and authority. They too saw the conquistadors as an opportunity to finally shrug off the authority and the demands of the Aztecs, and get revenge in a lot of ways.
Cortés builds up for the final campaign against Tenochtitlan, against the new huey tlatoani , Cuitláhuac, and then, after he died, his brother, the last of the huey tlatoanis , Cuauhtémoc . He has indigenous people flocking to his banner from all over Mesoamerica. He emphasizes that he’s still in the fight. He still has those advantages. More reinforcements are arriving at the coast, and they’re being integrated into the conquistador army. But of the force that marches on Tenochtitlan, the conquistadors were maybe 0.5% in raw numbers. The overwhelming majority were other Mesoamerican peoples who see this as their final reckoning with the Aztecs.
This is the integration again of the technology with the diplomacy. Cortés knows that to take the city, he has to control the lake. Again, the innovations… They need sulfur for their gunpowder. Their guns are useless without gunpowder. So an expedition goes into the mountains to the volcano, Popocatépetl , and they dangle with ropes inside the caldera of the volcano, scooping out the sulfur they need as the critical ingredient for their gunpowder.
Another one builds prefabricated brigantines, boats, in their different sections, and then has untold numbers of porters, human load-bearers, bring these prefabricated pieces over the mountains to the lake. They’re assembled and let loose in the lake, and now they have control over the lake. Bringing together the technology and the innovations with the local manpower, this is the force directed against Tenochtitlan in the final battle.

Dwarkesh Patel
There are so many interesting questions to ask, everything from technology to diplomacy to disease to tactics. Before we get to that, just to round out the story, in many ways what’s striking about the conquest of the Incas in Peru under Pizarro 10 years later is how similar it was to Cortés’s story, how much he just followed the playbook. I want people to have a sense of roughly what happened here, starting with the Battle of Cajamarca , which might be the most astonishingly lopsided military encounter that has maybe ever happened in human history. If you can recapitulate at least that battle a bit, and then the overall story, then we can do some analysis from there.
Si Sheppard
Absolutely. When Cortés consummates his conquest of Tenochtitlan in 1521, the last of the huey tlatoanis surrenders to him, and he occupies the territory and, most importantly, the top of the social hierarchy. The existing structure, as much as possible, is allowed to persist. The conquistadors just decapitate the local nobility and insert themselves at the top. Now, this is the playbook that will continue to be applied, less successfully, throughout the rest of Mesoamerica because the existing political entities there aren’t so sophisticated. Nonetheless, there are rumors — there are always rumors — of lost cities of gold. More conquistadors are now flooding into the New World to seek advantage of this.
One of these is a distant cousin of Cortés named Francisco Pizarro. He met with Cortés and got the playbook, as you say, about how this is to be done. Then, in one of these conquistador business ventures, he finds the funding for men and ships and begins pushing south down the west coast of South America, crossing the equator and finally arriving in what is now modern-day Peru. He encounters what he’s looking for: a sophisticated culture on the scale… in fact, in terms of territory, on a much broader scale than Cortés had encountered in Mesoamerica. This is the land of the Inca.
When he arrived, there was civil war . The previous Sapa Inca , Huayna Capac , had died in 1525, and his two sons, Atahualpa and Huáscar , turned on each other. Atahualpa had just won the civil war, and he was proceeding to Cuzco , the Inca capital, to be formally enthroned as the new Sapa Inca . That’s when word arrives to him that strangers have entered his realm. Again, we’re talking 168 men who have pushed into the Andes Mountains and are seeking him out for reasons unknown. He encounters them outside a place called, as you say, Cajamarca in 1532. He sees not threat, but opportunity.
Just like Moctezuma II had been advised not to treat with these people, so does Atahualpa’s envoy, who reports to him, “These are not men of good faith. We can take them out. They tend to sleep together. At night, we can set fire to wherever they’re staying and just take them all out in one fell swoop.”
But Atahualpa is interested in what they have to offer him. He’s seen the horses. In fact, Pizarro made a specific case of impressing the Sapa Inca with his horses. He sends one of his key lieutenants to put on a display of horsemanship. Famously, he charges right at the Sapa Inca and all his court. The horse stops just inches from Atahualpa’s face, snorting and breathing. Atahualpa is absolutely stony-faced and professes to be unimpressed and says, “I will meet with your master at a time of my choosing.” After the Spaniards leave, he has all of his court, including his generals who flinched or showed fear, executed just to make the point, “We’re not to be intimidated by these people.”
He sends a blocking force, tens of thousands of men, to cut off any retreat, and then he proceeds to meet with Pizarro at Cajamarca with the vast expanse of his army. There’s a saying in Peru that Atahualpa was planning to have Pizarro for lunch, but Pizarro ended up having him for breakfast. Once he enters into the confines of a narrow, constricted courtyard space, that’s when Pizarro lets off the ambush. He lets fly with all his artillery and all his musketry for the shock value, and unleashes his horses. Atahualpa himself is overwhelmed and taken prisoner at that point. This again is the playbook, the copybook, as you say. Go to the top. Seize whoever is at the very pinnacle of the social hierarchy. You control the entire structure.

Those societies that have arrived at the quick fix, whether that’s gold or silver or monocultural crop production of some kind which gives you a monopoly on its provision, become satisfied with that. They become trapped within an environment that exists solely for that purpose, and they don’t develop beyond the essential thresholds that that allows for.
Even here in the US, we can look at the difference between the North and the South, that geographic line. Below that line, yes, you can get the quick fix. You can put all your capital into buying slaves and have them work at providing cash crops: tobacco, the original one, indigo and sugar, and of course the big one, cotton. The North didn’t have those advantages. You couldn’t just buy land and then extract the resource from it. You had to think about how to make money. That’s why all the investment money in the North was going into things with long-term productivity: into trade and commerce, into advanced capital markets and the banking system, and of course into industrialization. That all happened in the North.
When the rupture came in the 1860s and the North and South went to war, all those advantages were with the North. It wasn’t just population, but the industrial base, the capital markets necessary for it to put in place a winning long-term strategy for actually defeating the South in that conflict. That again is the long-term lesson: having life too easy from the beginning leads to a certain… I don’t want to call it laziness, but lack of vision, perhaps, about alternatives that may, in the longer term, actually be more advantageous.
01:07:47 – Why didn’t the Aztecs/Incas adapt better to European warfare?
Dwarkesh Patel
Going back to the Aztecs and Incas, one thing that’s remarkable here is just how bad their strategy and tactics often were. For example, because Aztec military conflict was based around capturing victims for sacrifice, there are many cases where Cortés himself was lying on the ground, having been knocked off his horse, but instead of just killing him on the ground and pressing their advantage, the Aztecs were trying to seize him to go have him sacrificed later. This allowed him to get himself out of thorny situations.
Another huge mistake that would happen in these conflicts is that, in the traditional way in which these military conflicts would happen, the general would be garnished and plumed with all this extra decoration, which would make him easy to identify on a battlefield. If you have cavalry that can just see, “Here’s where the general is. Let’s go directly to him and kill him,” the whole enemy force will rout. It breaks your entire chain of command. You might have 100,000 troops, but then your general dies and everybody else routs.
Another example is the Battle of Cuzco : a couple hundred Spaniards defending against this huge rebellion. If the rebellion had just kept attacking at night instead of only attacking during the day, the Spaniards would have never gotten a chance to rest and would have been depleted pretty quickly. Instead, they would let them rest at night. They wouldn’t use their overwhelming numbers to do rotations and attack at different times.
Why I find this confusing is that it’s not like the conquistadors arrived at the doorsteps of a bunch of peace-loving hippies who didn’t know anything about war. Aztec society and Inca society were organized around warfare. All adult males were trained to be warriors. It was basically the Sparta of the New World. How do we understand how a very military-focused culture did not have the strategic wherewithal to do this improvisation? Cortés was doing improvisation. Pizarro was doing a bunch of improvisation. Why weren’t they able to do the same thing?
Si Sheppard
There are a lot of factors, one of which is that cultural and religious aspect to it. Warfare was rewarded by an increase in status. Warriors would be recognized for bringing captives back to Tenochtitlan for sacrifice. There was even a hierarchy in terms of the prisoners: four Totonacs were worth one Chichimec , because the Chichimec were a wilder tribe to the north and fought more fiercely than the Totonacs did.
To a degree, there’s a certain overlap with European cultures. There was great advantage throughout the feudal era in taking your enemies alive as opposed to killing them, because then you could ransom them back to their families. You could come out of that ahead in terms of at least financial gain. In a way, the Aztec model makes more sense, because sacrificing your enemy means you’ll never see him again, whereas selling your enemy back to his family means he’s up for round two. You might face off against the same guy across a future battlefield. Even kings… The kings of France after the Battles of Poitiers and Pavia were captured and then subject to various indignities. To a degree, there was a certain level of overlap.
More holistically, you’re right. Yes, the Aztecs did fight ideally to capture, not to kill. There were multiple episodes when they did have Cortés himself under their knives, but they were so desperate to haul him off that his compatriots, sometimes sacrificing themselves, were able to rescue him. That was an aspect of their warfare ingrained in their culture, in their faith, in the religious expression of the values of an entire people. It’s difficult to tilt that within the framework that we’re talking about. It’d be like asking people here, if we’re fighting aliens from another planet, to give up Christianity because there’s too much invested in terms of its morality, and more effective combat skills would be derived from following some other faith. It was not possible to reorient an entire society in that limited timeframe.
The Cuzco situation… To use a famous film example, the movie 300 . The Spartans are able to hold the pass at Thermopylae against overwhelming numbers because it’s very, very narrow. Within Cuzco, the surviving conquistadors fell back to two heavily fortified buildings with narrow entrances. The streets of Cuzco are quite narrow as well, which means that even those overwhelming numbers could only be channeled down through those narrow avenues. Those could be blocked relatively effectively, even by the small numbers of conquistadors and their increasing numbers of native allies, who with their metal armor were able to put in place blocking operations that could hold those kinds of frameworks. Beyond that, when the pressure came off, they would unleash their horses, largely through the wider plazas and then the surrounding countryside, looking for isolated detachments to take out.
This effect was demoralizing on the Inca. They initially had the advantage. They were rallied by Manco: “We’re going to deal with these barbarians once and for all.” As the frustration of the siege drags on, there are more and more defections, men slipping away at night either to switch sides or just go back to their homes and families. Again, this army is huge and very diverse, and many of those recruits are peoples who’ve been subjugated and have no institutional loyalty to the Inca. The bottom line is they need to get home to their farms to get the harvest in before the winter. The nature of warfare in pre-Columbian America, and in Europe for much of its history, was driven by the annual cycle of planting and harvesting the crops. You need the men on their farms to do that, because if they’re not there, you face a very hard year coming up if you don’t have enough crops set aside to get you through the winter and the following year.
For all those reasons, the Inca, yes, with those huge numbers, weren’t able to really overwhelmingly apply them at the same time. If they’d caught perhaps the entire conquistador army marching through one of those mountain passes, yes, they may have been able to win a set-piece battle in that environment. The horses can’t charge up the steep slopes. Both ends, if they’re blocked cleverly with boulders and with trees, mean the infantry’s not going anywhere either. You have them essentially pinned down. All the advantages would’ve been useless at that point. Metal armor won’t protect you from a two-ton boulder rumbling from the heights right into your face. On small scales they were able to arrange that. But to get the entire conquistador force in one place at one time like that never happened.
Dwarkesh Patel
So there was basically not enough time to accumulate the strategy?
Si Sheppard
Again, it’s that shockingly short, steep learning curve. There just wasn’t enough time, no.
01:14:16 – Conquistador motivation
Dwarkesh Patel
In retrospect, you have these outrageous battles with thousands of deaths on one side and zero deaths on the other side. My understanding is this came as a surprise to the conquistadors as well. How were some hundred-odd men willing to go confront an empire of 10 million people and say, “Your job is to take this over”? It’d be like dropping 100 Marines into 18th-century England and saying, “Okay, you better come out the other end with control over Parliament.” How is this a thing that they were willing to do?
Si Sheppard
The classic push-pull factors. They came from a proud land, a new country. Spain had only come together in 1492 , and they were aware their history was one of struggle, uniting the peninsula and consummating the Reconquista. They had a proud warrior background, but little to show for it, really. Again, the quality of life, particularly in Extremadura , which is the province in Spain where so many of these conquistadors originated from, was a land of hardship. It was small farms and dusty, unproductive terrain. They were aware of a wider world around them.
Like so many men throughout history, they see their only path forward to being anything more than dirt farmers as seeking a career of high risk and potentially even fatal outcomes, but with possible great reward as well. Men have joined armies and militaries and fought in campaigns, not even their own, even other peoples’, for many centuries, millennia now, for that reason.
Then there’s the attraction of the New World. Once they’re aware that out there is a whole new land where “it’s possible for even a small man like myself to become something, to become somebody. All I have as an asset is my fighting skills. Yeah, I could keep fighting in the campaigns against the Moors or against the Turks, but those are high-risk, low-return ventures. I’ll be fighting in my home waters, but what will the potential rewards be? These Turks can fight. The message I’m getting from the far side of the Atlantic is that the risks there are much lower and the potential rewards much higher. It means traveling across a vast ocean. It means essentially being very self-reliant and falling in with men who are just as cutthroat as I am.”
Dwarkesh Patel
How important is religion in the morale of these soldiers? In retrospect we say, “Okay, they were able to take on huge armies.” But they didn’t even know that there was an empire there to take over. They didn’t know where its capital was. They’d never seen it before. They knew nothing about what alliances they would need to maneuver, what size of armies they would need to face, what basic terrain and geography they would need to navigate. I’m still trying to understand how they gained the confidence — you and your couple hundred buddies — to go and take on a huge empire. Did they just think God would make sure that they were okay? How do we understand that?
Si Sheppard
Cortés and Pizarro both had men of the cloth traveling with their expeditions to provide for their spiritual needs. Cortés and Pizarro, in retrospect, would make a great deal of this in their writing. They professed to be outraged, particularly by the Aztec cult of human sacrifice, which was integral to their society. I don’t see any reason why that would be untrue. It would be shocking to actually see that happen. When Moctezuma invited Cortés to the top of one of the two great pyramids in Tenochtitlan, he remarks that the priests were daubed from head to foot in black clay or body paint, and the interior was black with spilled blood and stank abominably. I don’t see any reason why they wouldn’t be genuinely mortified by that.
They would take a certain degree of legitimacy from tearing down the ancient idols of these superstitious peoples and raising them up to the one true faith. You can salve your conscience in many ways by doing that: “In real terms I’m here for what I can take, but I am performing the will of God, and therefore I have some absolution when I’m called to account at the end of the day.” Men will sometimes seek to find a higher purpose in what are very low endeavors, which is what was playing out at that time.
Again, coming to the motivation, the initial conquests were relatively easy. The island populations in the Caribbean — what we now call Puerto Rico, Hispaniola, Cuba — were quite low on the tech tree , were not unified, and did not have a significant military presence. Of course, on islands there’s nowhere for them to run. You could run into the mountains of Cuba, but eventually they would all be hunted down. The feeling was, “The pickings here are pretty easy.” The enthusiastic attitude was, “Well, why would it be any different on the mainland?”
Of course, they did run up against heavy fighting, and some of them, veterans of wars against the Turks, would say afterwards, “This was some of the hardest fighting I’ve ever been through.” By that point they’d seen that the potential rewards were much higher as well. When they arrived at Lake Texcoco, they would marvel as if we had just arrived in Atlantis… to be in this place on this lake with these cities arranged around its edges, and the biggest city of all appearing to float on the water like Venice, except even more dramatic with its high temples. To them, this both awed them and inflamed that greed that they had.
Cortés himself admitted as much to Moctezuma’s envoys. He said, “My men and I suffer from a disease of the heart that only gold can cure.” So Moctezuma sent him feathered headdresses and all the traditional regalia. He sent him jade and obsidian, which in the Aztec world would have been symbols of great regard. Cortés was happy to put on the headdress, and he sent it back to the king. But the little trinkets of gold that he was getting, that was the real end purpose. The local peoples would talk about how the Spanish were chattering like monkeys as they were picking up this gold, fascinated by it.
Dwarkesh Patel
By the way, Bernal Díaz , who was on Cortés’s expedition and wrote a firsthand account of what happened afterwards, says in his account that in every single town they come across, even small outlying villages of the empire, every temple, every city has tons of human sacrifice remains left over. This is an endemic part of the society that I guess must have horrified the Spanish.
But it’s hard to square any sort of humanitarian, even plausible motivation to the Spanish. They were just very willing… I think maybe even worse than almost any other European conquest ever. The Spanish in the New World just massacre huge swaths of innocent women and children. Literal genocides, intentional genocides, not just disease. Crazy amounts of rape and assault. There wasn’t a sense of, “We’re freeing these people from the shackles of oppression,” or at least there’s no plausible story you could tell where that’s what you’re doing.
Si Sheppard
They would argue that in the long term it’s in these peoples’ interests. But of course in the short term, as you say, the accounts are unambiguous about this… throwing men and women to those huge hunting dogs that they brought with them for sport, to watch them being torn apart.
They could rationalize that in Mesoamerica in reference to that cult of sacrifice, which was endemic, ingrained into the mentalities of those people. There was a regular cycle of sacrifice, and that would peak according to particular crises, major campaigns, or the like. There are accounts — again, these are distorted accounts — of an almost industrial level of human sacrifice, that the parade of prisoners who’d been drugged to keep them pacified stretched from the pyramid complex all the way through to the causeways. One after the other they’d be brought up to the top of the pyramids to the temple complex. Four priests, each would take an arm and a leg, while a fifth one would use an obsidian blade to open an incision and pull out the heart, which they’d offer to the gods.
I’ve been to those pyramids, the ones at Teotihuacan. The steps leading up are very, very narrow. You almost have to walk up sideways, deliberately so, because when the heart had been taken out and burned, the body would be tumbled off the far side, and the steps were so steep to allow it to tumble all the way to the bottom. Then the warrior who’d taken that prisoner would take the flesh home to be consumed. It was the consummation of his victory as a warrior to take home, literally internalize, the defeated enemy.
This was, as you say, a cycle that the world is better off without. I’m not going to make any excuses for it. But the motivations of those who did that were not selfless. It was at best a beneficial side effect, window dressing on a process of colonization and even literal genocide. It had even less validation in South America, because there was no such tradition at all. The Inca would very occasionally take a virgin sacrifice into the high mountains where she’d be drugged, and you can still find the mummified corpses of these girls, but that was very rare and very specific. There was no ingrained culture of mass human sacrifice at all.
01:26:22 – Why Mughal India also couldn’t play the Europeans off each other
Dwarkesh Patel
Here’s a question I find very interesting. Why is it that the Spaniards are able to weaponize the divisions between the different native forces to their own advantage, but the natives are not able to do the same with the Spaniards? We know there were significant divisions among the Spaniards. Literally, there’s a whole much larger force that is sent over to arrest Cortés. Similarly, we didn’t get into this much, but in Pizarro’s expedition, his second in command, Almagro , is pissed off that Pizarro is getting all the spoils. He comes back, lays siege to Cuzco after Manco Inca has laid siege to Cuzco, and captures it.
They were literally fighting, in multiple cases in both these expeditions. The Spanish are fighting each other while they’re taking over a much larger empire. Why is it that Manco Inca or Moctezuma are not able to use these divisions to their own advantage to stay in power?
Si Sheppard
That’s a good question. The critical difference is this is happening for the conquistadors within the Spanish orbit. They’re all Castilians , they’re all Spaniards.
d common backgrounds for most of them, and their confrontations were personal rivalries within their system. If confronted by an existential crisis, to pit them against each other, I think that would have failed. Their bottom line was they would have united if they felt there was a common cause against them that would have forced them to do that.
Whereas for the local peoples, these are differences between entire societies, both the external and internal factors here. Cuitláhuac, who succeeded his brother, Moctezuma II, as huey tlatoani after driving the Spaniards out during La Noche Triste, sent emissaries to the Tlaxcalans to build and expand their own new triple alliance, to come together as native people with a common interest against these outsiders. The Tlaxcalan younger generation thought about it. But the old men who still retained power, who had been fighting against the Aztecs for decades and saw them as, in the end, the existential threat, repudiated this possibility. The Tlaxcalans wanted nothing to do with the Aztecs. “No, we are enemies and always have been and always will be. This is your problem, and maybe you’ll get taken out. No loss to us.” They couldn’t overcome the kind of institutional hostilities which go way, way back through their history.
The only way that the indigenous people could have played the same game — and to a degree this did happen later — was to play different European powers off against each other. But of course the problem for the Aztecs, for the Inca, was the Spanish were the only game in town. They were the only outsiders they’d encountered. If the English, French, Dutch, even the Ottomans had been players in New World colonization at that very early stage, it may have been possible for local peoples to identify, “Okay, these are different cultures. We can play them off against each other to our advantage.” That may have been the game, but at that time, it wasn’t possible.
Dwarkesh Patel
I know you’ve spent a little bit of time thinking about what happened in all these other episodes of colonialism as well. In India, for example, we do have many different European powers. Why was it not possible for the Mughals to play them off each other indefinitely to keep control over India?
Si Sheppard
The Mughals peaked under Aurangzeb at the beginning of the 18th century. Subsequently, his successors didn’t have whatever you call it — the authority, the charisma, the rizz, however you want to define it — to maintain that hegemony which he’d established by almost taking over all of India.
The Mughals always at root had the same problem that the Manchu did when they conquered the Ming in the 17th century and built Qing dynasty China to the greatest physical extent it ever had. They were always outsiders. They were not indigenous to the population. In the background, there was always that vague resentment that “we’re being ruled here by foreigners.” That was okay while they were going from success to success. But when the momentum halts and it begins to roll backwards, that’s when the local power brokers begin to feel to themselves, “Maybe we have more to gain from breaking away from the empire than we do from remaining within it.” Both Mughal India and Manchu China had that kind of fatal defect, as it were, coded into the ultimate basis for their authority.
So even without the Europeans, I think the Mughal Empire would have peaked and it would have disintegrated. What would have happened next would be more like a patchwork quilt of different authorities, some larger, some smaller. The Maratha Confederacy I think would have been a major player, but not one that could incorporate the entire subcontinent within its authority.
Of course, we look at Chinese history. It’s the constant cycle of the rise of a dynasty, its peak, its decline into anarchy, and then the rise of a new imperial hegemon in the region. That cycle would play out, I think, regardless of whether the Europeans intervened.
Specifically in terms of why the British took over at the end, in the bluntest terms, the Royal Navy rules the waves. In terms of projecting power, the Brits were able to force the French out of Canada and out of India because they controlled the sea lanes. Although the French had, in terms of population and military, a larger force, if you can’t project that force, it’s really of no value to you.
Dwarkesh Patel
This is very interesting, because it makes sense why the British could have been one of the major powers in India, where there’s the Marathas, there’s the remainders of the Mughal Empire, there’s many other factions, and then there’s this British outpost. They’ve gotten rid of the French, the British have got Bengal . Why is it that they were able to consolidate where nobody else is able to consolidate?
One hypothesis here is that we often talk about advantages in technology, but we don’t talk about advantages in governance or in terms of organization. The East India Company was a company. It had a board that could fire and hire CEOs and lieutenants and whatever. It wasn’t called a CEO, obviously. It wasn’t necessarily the most competent people who were in charge of the East India Trading Company, though they were pretty competent people, Robert Clive and his underlings.
You would have instances… For example, I think the nawabs of Bengal right before the British are able to gain control of Bengal, which is the initial tax base that they set up in order to further their conquest. Alivardi Khan is considered to be a pretty good ruler who was able to hold the British at bay. Then he dies, and his grandson is this kind of crazy, psychopathic guy who doesn’t listen to advice and so forth. Because there’s this monarchical tradition across all these principalities in India, there’s just going to be, sooner or later, a bad king, and that king will not be able to stop the British incursion.
There are also all these forces around these principalities where it’s, “Who are we going to lend money to? Who are we going to lend our troops to?” They realize there’s a bad king right now. The British seem pretty competent. “I’m going to make an alliance with the British instead.”
Similarly with the case of the Aztecs and the Incas, and maybe even more so, the overwhelming majority of the troops, the forces that the British are using are Indians, sepoys . This is an underrated thing, being able to delegate to competent people and then being able to replace them one after another if they get killed or if they resign or something. There’s not going to be some crazy succession crisis that creates this anarchy that then somebody else can swoop in and take control.
Si Sheppard
Absolutely. Just to give one example, the British were winning the Hundred Years’ War because the King of France, Charles VI , was insane. He was convinced he was made out of glass , for example. Then the English lost the Hundred Years’ War because their king, Henry VI , was insane, and he would just shut down for months at a time. Mentally, he just couldn’t function.
In non-democratic environments, yes, the corporations, for the reasons you outlined — profit-driven and therefore not loyal to bloodlines or any other kind of extraneous circumstances, but just looking for the most competent leadership and a capacity to change and replace people — are much more flexible than those kind of hierarchical states. Even in many ways, you could argue, in democratic societies the corporations can still outmaneuver them because they’re untied to particular loyalties, and it’s just their brand awareness that they’re trying to push.
The British and Dutch were late to the game of imperialism. You wouldn’t give them much of a chance given the differentials, but they unleashed the power of the free market. As you say, the East India Company, the English and Dutch equivalents , the British Hudson’s Bay Company , which is really what made Canada as we understand it today… Those corporations, driven by those motives, acting with almost plenipotentiary powers at vast distances, had the flexibility on the ground to take advantage of local circumstances, to insert themselves into diplomatic arrangements which had persisted for centuries with all the accumulated grievances. Here were the fresh newcomers: “We obviously have skills at what we do.”
As you say, taking control of the tax revenue of Bengal effectively took control of the entire political orbit. What can you do without taxation? Reorient that towards the
holders, force the local people to stop producing these crops, even if it means millions starve to death, in order to produce opium to take advantage of that. It was devastating in its impact in political structures that had no real environment where that was part of their established traditions.
Even the conquistadors, even the Vikings, if we go back far enough, these were business operations. The Vikings would be men who would assemble with what weapons they had, enough capital upfront to pay for boats to go raiding, to make this profitable by looting churches and monasteries.
That’s what the conquistadors were. They were freebooters operating within a capitalist system. These were not desperados of the lowest of the low. Many of them had some education. A lot of them were hidalgos , which is the lowest rung of the petty nobility in Castile, who inherited titles and attitudes, but not much in assets beyond what they could put up as soldiers of fortune. They called themselves compañeros , which is colleagues or companions, not soldados . This whole operation was, if you like, the most raw, unbridled capitalism at its finest, pooling assets to derive enormous profits from very risky ventures.
01:33:42 – Diplomacy was just as important as technology
Dwarkesh Patel
Maybe to close us off, the thing I find really remarkable in these stories… We’ve talked about how lopsided these battles have been, how quickly this conquest proceeded. In the grand scheme of things, the technological advantages that the conquistadors had over these empires are not that shockingly big. As you mentioned, guns at this time are not good enough to actually be that helpful during battle, so it’s basically horses and steel weapons. In fact, I don’t know if we’ve talked about this, but towards the ends of these conquests, or the middles of these conquests, the metal is weighing them down and it’s really hot and stuff. So they’re even reverting to the cotton armor that the natives are using. So it’s not even the armor.
It’s really just that they had better metal swords instead of obsidian blades, and that they had horses. That’s enough to let a couple hundred men take over entire empires. You can draw analogies to what might happen in the future. We don’t even have to go there. I just find it remarkable how little it took for them to be so dominant.
Si Sheppard
They could win battles. They could not have won the war, though, at the end without the support of those indigenous allies. So those are all the numbers. Yes, you’re actually right. They would almost keep the helmets to protect the head, but many of them were ditching the metal armor in favor of the local stuff because that was good enough as protection against local weapons. They would not have done that and carried it back to Europe. That would have made them too vulnerable. But here you adapt to the circumstances. You have more freedom of mobility. It’s less exhausting wearing cotton than it is to wear metal. They would make that adaptation.
They would even sometimes, for very prized allies, again as a way of integrating them into the Spanish coalition, provide local chiefs and warlords with Spanish weapons, just to emphasize, “You’re so special to me.” This was effective in binding them together. They were very, very clever in identifying the local sub-chiefs beneath the Aztec layer of authority who would be receptive to the idea of, “Why are you taking orders from them when you get a better deal from us?”
They knew, for example, the Aztec Empire was called the Triple Alliance because it was set up in 1428 by the first huey tlatoani as a coalition between the three city-states: Tenochtitlan, Texcoco , and Tacuba.
In 1516, so three years before Cortés arrived in Mesoamerica, the chief, or king, of Texcoco died, and his two sons fought a civil war for who would succeed him. Moctezuma stepped in and gave the title to one of those brothers. So what does the other brother do when the Spanish show up? He immediately sides with them. His own family was on both sides of this war. This is his path to getting what he feels is his due, being the king of Texcoco. In the broader sense, “If I’m under the Spanish versus under the Aztecs, maybe these newcomers will give me a better deal.” There was that seductive quality at the elite level.
Now, what they didn’t understand — so many of them thinking they were using the Spanish to their advantage — they didn’t appreciate the numbers in which the Spanish and later Europeans would arrive. Their closed worlds didn’t allow them to think that people from such vast distances could come in such huge numbers. In the end, as happened in, for example, Australia and New Zealand, “We’ll become the minority in our own country.” The demographic tide they didn’t appreciate in the long term would work fundamentally against them.
Dwarkesh Patel
The analogy to AI there is so obvious it’s not worth spelling out. But that’s actually an excellent place to close. This is such a bizarre and shocking and interesting part of human history, and I appreciate you coming on and telling us all about it.
Si Sheppard
Real pleasure. Thank you for having me.
